## PURPOSE

The Child Witness Specialist is a specialized role for situations in which a child may be affected by:

- serious conflict
- family separation
- violence
- abuse
- safeguarding concerns
- justice processes
- institutional intervention
- other situations where the child's perspective requires particular protection

The role exists to help ensure that the child is:

- heard without being burdened
- protected without being silenced
- respected without being recruited into adult conflict
- able to participate without being forced to perform
- treated as a person rather than as evidence

---

# 1. THIS IS A SPECIALIST ROLE

The Child Witness Specialist is **not simply a Youth Journey Facilitator with additional training**.

It is a distinct specialist function.

It may interface with:

- child protection
- medicine
- psychology
- education
- social services
- legal systems
- safeguarding professionals
- emergency services
- other external institutions

The role therefore cannot be treated as a self-contained Flow authority.

Where external professional or legal requirements apply, those requirements must be followed according to the relevant jurisdiction and current professional standards.

Flow does not replace:

- medical professionals
- licensed mental-health professionals
- statutory child-protection authorities
- courts
- police
- legal counsel
- other legally defined professional functions

---

# 2. CORE PRINCIPLE

> **The child is a person, not a case.**

The child must never become merely:

- evidence
- a messenger
- a witness recruited by one adult against another
- a justification for adult decisions
- a source of emotional comfort
- a symbol of family honour
- a tool for proving someone's innocence or guilt

The specialist protects the child's personhood and agency within the limits of safety, law and professional responsibility.

---

# 3. AUTONOMY WITHIN BOUNDARIES

Children have agency.

That agency is not identical to adult legal autonomy.

The specialist must therefore work within:

- the child's developmental capacity
- the child's expressed wishes
- safeguarding requirements
- legal requirements
- medical requirements
- constitutional boundaries
- professional standards

The child should be offered meaningful choices wherever possible.

Examples:

- whether to speak
- whether to remain silent
- who should be present
- whether to take a break
- how to communicate
- whether to use drawing, writing or another medium
- whether to request a different support person
- whether to continue a non-mandatory conversation

A child should not be told that their choice is unlimited when the specialist knows that legal or safeguarding obligations constrain it.

Honest boundaries protect trust.

---

# 4. THE CHILD DOES NOT HAVE TO RESOLVE THE ADULTS' CONFLICT

The specialist must never place responsibility for adult repair on the child.

The child is not responsible for:

- reconciling parents
- forgiving adults
- proving abuse
- protecting an adult's feelings
- choosing sides
- deciding punishment
- deciding whether adults remain together
- carrying confidential information between adults

A child may express preferences.

Those preferences should be taken seriously.

They should not automatically be transformed into adult decisions.

---

# 5. WITNESSING WITHOUT EXTRACTION

The specialist should not pressure a child to disclose more than is necessary.

Avoid:

- repeated questioning without purpose
- suggestive questions
- emotionally leading questions
- pressure to remember details
- pressure to produce a coherent narrative
- rewarding particular answers
- implying disbelief through repeated interrogation

The specialist should distinguish between:

- what the child spontaneously says
- what the child says in response to a question
- what the adult believes the child meant
- what the specialist observes
- what has been independently established

---

# 6. BODY AND BEHAVIOUR

The specialist may observe:

- posture
- movement
- silence
- facial expression
- breathing
- changes in behaviour
- withdrawal
- agitation
- play
- drawing
- apparent distress

These observations may provide useful additional information.

They are not proof of:

- truth
- deception
- trauma
- guilt
- innocence
- a particular diagnosis
- a particular event

Use:

> **Body = observation / signal / additional information.**

Do not use:

> "The body cannot lie."

Do not use:

> "The nervous system is the primary witness."

Do not treat physiological or behavioural responses as a substitute for careful evidence.

---

# 7. REGULATION

A child may become overwhelmed during a conversation.

The specialist may offer:

- a pause
- water
- quiet
- movement
- a different communication method
- a support person
- sensory adaptation
- a change of environment

The purpose is to support safe participation.

Regulation should not be used to manipulate a child's testimony.

A child does not have to become calm before their words can be taken seriously.

Similarly, distress does not by itself establish the truth of a particular allegation.

---

# 8. CHILD'S RIGHT TO STOP

Where the process is voluntary, the child may:

- stop
- pause
- leave
- change their mind
- refuse a question
- request another person
- communicate nonverbally

Where participation is legally required, the specialist must explain the relevant limitation honestly and age-appropriately.

The specialist must never falsely promise:

> "You can always leave."

if they know that the process has legal constraints.

---

# 9. SILENCE

Silence is allowed.

Silence may mean many things.

It should not automatically be interpreted as:

- consent
- refusal
- guilt
- innocence
- fear
- deception
- trauma

The specialist may say:

> "You don't have to answer that right now."

where the procedural context permits.

---

# 10. COMMUNICATION

Children may communicate through:

- speech
- writing
- drawing
- gesture
- play
- assistive communication
- interpreters
- communication devices
- other accessible methods

The communication method should not determine the credibility assigned to the child.

Accessibility is part of the evidentiary environment.

A child who communicates differently must not be treated as less credible merely because the system is less familiar with their communication style.

---

# 11. ACCESSIBILITY

The specialist must understand accessibility as part of participation and safeguarding.

Possible needs include:

- mobility access
- sensory adaptation
- communication assistance
- interpreter support
- AAC
- rest
- medication
- medical equipment
- predictable structure
- reduced sensory load
- extra processing time

The child should not have to prove disability in order to receive reasonable accommodation.

---

# 12. CONFLICT OF INTEREST

The specialist must consider whether they have:

- personal relationships with involved adults
- financial interests
- ideological commitments
- previous involvement that compromises independence
- emotional investment in a particular outcome

If the conflict is significant, the specialist should recuse themselves.

A child should never have to trust a specialist who is simultaneously advocating for an adult opponent.

---

# 13. ADULT COMMUNICATION

The specialist should not use the child as an intermediary.

Do not ask:

> "Can you tell your mother that..."

Do not ask:

> "What does your father say about this?"

unless such information is genuinely necessary within an appropriately defined professional process.

Adults should communicate directly through the appropriate channel.

---

# 14. SAFEGUARDING

When a safeguarding concern arises, the specialist must follow the relevant safeguarding pathway.

This may require contacting:

- child-protection services
- emergency services
- medical professionals
- police
- legal professionals
- designated safeguarding personnel

The specialist should not promise secrecy that cannot legally or ethically be maintained.

Instead:

> "I will respect your privacy as much as I can. If I believe you are in serious danger, there are things I may have to tell another person so that you can be safe. I will explain what I can."

Exact wording should be adapted to age, jurisdiction and professional context.

---

# 15. DOCUMENTATION

Documentation is governance infrastructure.

It must be:

- minimal
- purpose-bound
- transparent in scope
- access-limited
- contestable
- subject to expiry where appropriate

Child-related documentation requires particular care.

The specialist should distinguish:

### Direct observation
What the specialist personally observed.

### Child statement
What the child communicated.

### Adult statement
What another person communicated.

### Professional assessment
An assessment made within appropriate professional competence.

### Interpretation
A hypothesis or contextual interpretation.

### Procedural action
What was done.

These categories must not be collapsed.

---

# 16. DOCUMENTATION MUST NOT BECOME PERMANENT IDENTITY

A child should not acquire a permanent institutional identity because of one incident.

Records should not casually turn:

- "child appeared distressed"

into:

- "child is traumatized"

or:

- "child is unreliable"

or:

- "child is a victim"

unless such a designation is legitimately established within an appropriate professional or legal framework.

Documentation should describe what is known, what is reported, what is uncertain and what action was taken.

---

# 17. EXTERNAL PROFESSIONAL INTERFACES

The Child Witness Specialist may work alongside external professionals.

The specialist must clearly identify which function they are performing.

For example:

> Flow witness support

is not the same as:

> clinical assessment

and neither is the same as:

> forensic interviewing

and neither is the same as:

> legal representation

Role boundaries must remain visible.

Flow must not create an informal substitute for regulated professional expertise merely by giving a person a specialist title.

---

# 18. TRAINING

The specialist role requires additional training beyond ordinary facilitation.

Training should include:

### Child development
- developmental differences
- communication
- autonomy
- developmental capacity

### Safeguarding
- abuse
- neglect
- exploitation
- coercion
- reporting duties
- safety planning

### Trauma-aware practice
- effects of overwhelming experiences
- regulation
- boundaries
- avoiding retraumatizing procedures

### Communication
- non-leading questions
- accessible communication
- AAC
- interpreters
- child-friendly explanation

### Legal and professional interfaces
- jurisdiction-specific requirements
- confidentiality limits
- mandatory reporting
- evidence boundaries
- referral pathways

### Documentation
- minimal necessary records
- access controls
- retention
- correction mechanisms
- uncertainty

### Specialist supervision
- case review
- ethical dilemmas
- role boundaries
- secondary stress
- recusal

---

# 19. SUPERVISED PRACTICE

No person should enter the specialist role solely because they completed theoretical training.

Specialist practice should include:

- supervised cases
- observed practice
- structured feedback
- competency assessment
- continuing education
- regular supervision

Exact training hours should be determined according to the competence required and relevant professional standards.

Numbers should not be treated as proof of competence by themselves.

---

# 20. PROFESSIONAL VALIDATION

Clinical, legal and safeguarding procedures must be validated against:

- the relevant jurisdiction
- current legislation
- current professional standards
- relevant regulatory requirements
- current evidence

Flow documentation must not present an internally developed procedure as legally or clinically authoritative merely because it appears in the repository.

---

# 21. WHEN THE SPECIALIST MUST STOP

The specialist should stop or transfer the case when:

- the matter exceeds their competence
- immediate safety requires emergency intervention
- legal requirements take precedence
- clinical assessment is required
- the specialist's independence is compromised
- the child requires a different communication specialist
- the specialist can no longer maintain appropriate boundaries

Transfer is not failure.

It is competent practice.

---

# 22. THE CHILD IS NOT THE SYSTEM'S PROPERTY

The child does not belong to:

- Flow
- the facilitator
- the family
- the justice process
- the documentation system
- the institution

The specialist's responsibility is therefore not to make the child fit the system.

The responsibility is to make the system respond appropriately to the child.

---

# 23. CORE RULE

> **Protect the child without possessing the child.**

Listen without extracting.

Support without directing.

Observe without pretending observation is proof.

Document without turning temporary circumstances into permanent identity.

Intervene when safety requires it.

Step aside when another competence is required.

And never make the child responsible for resolving the world of the adults around them.
