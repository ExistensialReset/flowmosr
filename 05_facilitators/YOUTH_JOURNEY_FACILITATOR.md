**Role:** Temporary Youth Journey Facilitation

## PURPOSE

A Youth Journey Facilitator supports young people during shared journeys, exchanges and encounters.

The facilitator exists to help make participation:

- safe
- accessible
- voluntary
- meaningful
- culturally respectful
- developmentally appropriate

The facilitator does **not** exist to control the journey or to determine what the young people should experience.

The purpose of the role is:

> **Young people should return with greater autonomy, competence and freedom — not greater dependence on the facilitator.**

---

# 1. ROLE BOUNDARIES

A Youth Journey Facilitator is:

- a safety anchor
- a procedural guide
- an accessibility support
- a cultural bridge
- a conflict-sensitive adult presence
- a temporary holder of bounded procedural power

A Youth Journey Facilitator is **not**:

- a parent by default
- a teacher
- a therapist
- a judge
- a permanent authority
- a spokesperson for all youth
- the owner of the journey
- the person who decides what a young person "really needs"

The facilitator's authority belongs to the **function**, not to the individual.

---

# 2. BOUNDED PROCEDURAL POWER

Facilitators may temporarily exercise procedural power when necessary to fulfil the role.

Examples include:

- stopping an activity because of an immediate safety concern
- changing logistics when necessary for accessibility or safety
- separating participants when continued contact creates risk
- requiring a pause
- contacting emergency or professional support when necessary
- enforcing agreed process boundaries
- preventing bullying, coercion or exploitation

This power is bounded.

The facilitator may **not**:

- punish participants
- humiliate participants
- impose personal beliefs
- override constitutional rights
- compel emotional disclosure
- force reconciliation
- determine a person's identity or experience for them
- use the role to create personal authority
- extend their own mandate without review

The facilitator must be able to explain:

1. what procedural power was used;
2. why it was necessary;
3. what boundary authorized it;
4. how long it lasted;
5. whether it can be challenged or reviewed.

---

# 3. TEMPORARY ROLE

Facilitation is a role, not an identity.

Facilitators should be:

- temporary
- replaceable
- reviewable
- accountable
- subject to rotation

A facilitator must be able to step down without shame.

A system that requires one person to remain indispensable has failed to distribute responsibility.

---

# 4. YOUTH AUTONOMY

Young people are participants, not objects of management.

Facilitators should therefore distinguish between:

- safety requirements
- accessibility requirements
- legal/professional requirements
- group agreements
- facilitator preferences

These are not equivalent.

A facilitator should not describe a personal preference as a safety requirement.

Youth should have meaningful opportunities to:

- ask questions
- disagree
- decline optional activities
- request adaptations
- request support
- request distance
- propose changes
- leave optional activities
- participate in decisions appropriate to their age and circumstances

Where a restriction is necessary, the facilitator should explain its basis as clearly as possible.

---

# 5. SAFETY

Safety is the primary procedural responsibility of the role.

Potential risks include:

- physical injury
- medical emergencies
- abuse
- exploitation
- sexual harm
- bullying
- coercion
- disappearance or unsafe separation
- inaccessible environments
- environmental hazards
- serious psychological distress

Safety intervention should be:

- proportionate
- necessary
- as limited as possible
- documented when consequential
- reviewed when appropriate

Safety does not justify unnecessary control.

---

# 6. ACCESSIBILITY

Accessibility is not an exception granted to individual participants.

The journey should be designed so that different bodies, sensory systems, communication styles and support needs can participate.

Facilitators should consider:

- mobility
- fatigue
- chronic conditions
- sensory environments
- communication access
- medication and medical equipment
- dietary requirements
- rest
- transport
- accessible accommodation
- unpredictable changes in capacity

The relevant question is not:

> "Can this person keep up with the standard journey?"

It is:

> "How can the journey be adapted so this person can participate safely and meaningfully?"

Disabled people should have meaningful influence over accessibility design.

---

# 7. CULTURAL HUMILITY

Facilitators do not become experts on another culture merely by studying it.

They should:

- ask rather than assume
- recognize their own cultural position
- avoid treating difference as deficiency
- support translation and communication
- distinguish disagreement from danger
- repair mistakes openly

Cultural difference is not itself a safety problem.

At the same time, cultural context does not remove constitutional protections against coercion, abuse or serious harm.

---

# 8. CONFLICT

The facilitator may help participants navigate ordinary conflict.

The facilitator should distinguish:

- disagreement
- misunderstanding
- interpersonal conflict
- repeated harmful behaviour
- coercion
- serious violence
- safeguarding concerns

Not every conflict requires mediation.

Not every relationship should be repaired.

Separation can sometimes be the appropriate outcome.

The facilitator should never force reconciliation merely because dialogue is available.

---

# 9. REGULATION AND PARTICIPATION

Facilitators may observe signs that someone is highly activated, withdrawn, overwhelmed or otherwise having difficulty participating.

Such observations are **signals, not diagnoses**.

Examples may include:

- rapid speech
- silence
- withdrawal
- agitation
- changes in breathing
- muscular tension
- difficulty concentrating
- sudden confusion
- repetitive speech
- visible distress

These observations may indicate that a pause, quieter environment, support person or other accommodation would be useful.

However:

> **A bodily signal is not proof of what happened, what someone intends, or whether someone's account is true.**

Dysregulation can affect:

- attention
- memory
- communication
- reflection
- decision-making
- capacity to participate safely

Regulation may support:

- attention
- communication
- reflection
- participation

A regulated person can still be mistaken.

A dysregulated person can still tell the truth.

Facilitators must not treat calmness as evidence of innocence or distress as evidence of deception.

---

# 10. WHEN TO PAUSE

A facilitator may pause a process when:

- someone requests a pause
- safety becomes uncertain
- communication has broken down
- someone cannot participate voluntarily
- accessibility needs are not currently met
- the process itself is becoming harmful
- additional support is required

A pause is not automatically a failure.

Sometimes stopping is the correct facilitation decision.

---

# 11. PARENTAL AND FAMILY CONFLICTS OF INTEREST

A parent or close relative may participate as part of a facilitation team where appropriate.

They should not normally be the sole facilitator for their own child.

Reasons include:

- conflict of interest
- unequal emotional proximity
- difficulty separating family roles from facilitation
- the child's need for relationships outside parental authority

The relevant question is not whether parents are inherently unsuitable.

It is whether the role structure protects the child from role conflict and unequal procedural power.

---

# 12. SELECTION

Potential facilitators may be selected through:

1. self-nomination;
2. relevant experience review;
3. peer assessment;
4. supervised participation;
5. training;
6. practical assessment;
7. temporary certification.

Selection should examine:

- respect for youth autonomy
- ability to maintain boundaries
- safety awareness
- accessibility competence
- capacity to work across difference
- ability to recognize personal limits
- willingness to be challenged
- ability to relinquish procedural control

A facilitator should not be selected merely because they are charismatic, respected or socially dominant.

---

# 13. TRAINING

Initial training should cover at minimum:

### Module 1 — Youth Development and Autonomy
- developmental differences
- age-appropriate participation
- youth rights
- boundaries
- autonomy and safety

### Module 2 — Safety and Emergency Response
- risk assessment
- emergency communication
- first aid appropriate to the role
- evacuation
- safeguarding

### Module 3 — Accessibility
- mobility access
- sensory access
- communication access
- medical equipment
- fatigue and variable capacity
- universal design

### Module 4 — Cross-Cultural Facilitation
- cultural humility
- language barriers
- power differences
- repair after misunderstanding

### Module 5 — Conflict and De-escalation
- ordinary conflict
- boundary setting
- separation
- voluntary dialogue
- escalation pathways

### Module 6 — Child Protection
- recognizing safeguarding concerns
- responding to disclosures
- mandatory reporting where applicable
- external professional interfaces

### Module 7 — Documentation
- incident documentation
- minimal necessary records
- confidentiality
- access limitation
- retention and expiry
- reviewability

---

# 14. DOCUMENTATION

Documentation is governance infrastructure.

It must be:

- minimal
- purpose-bound
- transparent in scope
- access-limited
- contestable
- subject to expiry where appropriate

Facilitators should not collect information merely because it might someday be useful.

Documentation should distinguish:

- observation
- participant statement
- interpretation
- professional assessment
- decision
- uncertainty

Records should not transform facilitator impressions into permanent facts.

---

# 15. ESCALATION

A Youth Journey Facilitator must know when the matter exceeds the role.

Escalation may involve:

- another facilitator
- a safeguarding function
- Refugium Anima
- medical services
- emergency services
- child protection
- legal authorities
- specialist practitioners

The facilitator should not improvise professional or legal procedures.

Clinical, legal and safeguarding procedures must be validated against the relevant jurisdiction and current professional standards.

---

# 16. STEP-DOWN CONDITIONS

A facilitator should step down when:

- personal involvement compromises the role
- they cannot maintain procedural fairness
- they are overwhelmed
- they have a conflict of interest
- the case exceeds their competence
- another facilitator is better positioned
- their mandate expires

Stepping down is not failure.

It is part of responsible power limitation.

---

# 17. CORE RULE

> **The facilitator holds the process temporarily so that young people can increasingly hold their own lives.**

The goal is not obedient youth.

The goal is not facilitator dependence.

The goal is greater autonomy, safety, participation and capacity.
