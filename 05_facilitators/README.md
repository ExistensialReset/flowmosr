# 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Flow Facilitators

Holding space without controlling it

This folder defines the role of facilitators in Flow.

Not leaders.
Not personal authorities.

Facilitators do not hold personal sovereignty over other people.

They may, however, temporarily hold bounded procedural power where necessary to protect safety, accessibility and the integrity of a process.

The power belongs to the function, not to the individual.

Documentation is governance infrastructure. It must be minimal, purpose-bound, transparent in scope, access-limited, contestable and subject to expiry where appropriate.

---

🧭 What this is

Facilitators exist to:

- Maintain clarity in group processes
- Support communication
- Protect psychological and relational safety
- Support accessibility and meaningful participation
- Protect the conditions in which people can think and act freely

Facilitators do not:

- Make decisions for others
- Determine people's identities, experiences or moral worth
- Define outcomes in advance
- Exercise personal authority over participants
- Turn temporary procedural power into permanent power

---

⚖️ Bounded Procedural Power

Facilitation can involve real procedural power.

This may include the ability to:

- pause or stop a process
- establish procedural boundaries
- prevent intimidation or domination
- separate participants when necessary for safety
- request additional support or expertise
- make consequential procedural interventions

Such power must remain:

- limited
- necessary
- proportionate
- temporary
- explainable
- challengeable
- reviewable

«Facilitators hold power over the process, not sovereignty over the people in it.»

The facilitator must be able to explain:

1. what power was used;
2. why it was necessary;
3. what boundary authorized it;
4. how long it applies;
5. how it can be challenged or reviewed.

---

🧭 Core Constraint

«A facilitator must never become necessary.»

If a system depends on a person to function:

→ It is not stable
→ Responsibility has not been sufficiently distributed
→ The system must be redesigned

Facilitation is a function, not an identity.

No facilitator should become indispensable.

Role safety should be assessed by relevant present capacity and behavior, not by diagnostic category.

---

🔄 Role Characteristics

Facilitation should be:

- Rotational
- Voluntary where the role permits
- Transparent
- Replaceable
- Reviewable
- Temporary

A facilitator must be able to step down without losing dignity or standing.

Stepping down is part of responsible power limitation.

---

🧩 Role Boundaries

Different facilitation roles may serve different functions.

These may include:

- general process facilitation
- youth journey facilitation
- justice facilitation
- specialist child-witness functions
- witness and sanctuary functions
- other clearly defined temporary roles

Different roles are functional distinctions, not ranks.

A specialist role does not create general authority over other participants.

Where a role interfaces with regulated professional, legal or safeguarding functions, those external requirements remain applicable.

Flow does not replace professional or statutory authority merely by creating an internal role.

---

🧠 What Lives Here

- Facilitation protocols
- Youth facilitation
- Justice and conflict-process structures
- Witness and sanctuary functions
- Specialist facilitation roles
- Competence and certification protocols
- Group process guidance

Each role should clearly define:

- its purpose
- its permitted procedural power
- its limits
- its escalation pathways
- its review mechanisms
- its conditions for stepping down

---

🌊 The Principle

Facilitators do not exist to control people.

They exist to protect the conditions in which people can participate freely.

«Holding the process temporarily so that people can increasingly hold their own lives.»

The goal is not facilitator dependence.

The goal is not obedience.

The goal is not invisible authority.

The goal is that responsibility can be carried without becoming ownership.

---

💙 A Small Reminder

The best facilitation may become almost invisible.

When it works, people do not feel that someone is leading their lives.

They feel that the conditions for participation are being protected.

And when the conditions no longer require the facilitator:

the facilitator steps away.
