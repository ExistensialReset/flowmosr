## PURPOSE

The Justice Facilitator holds the structure of restorative and protective justice processes.

The facilitator is not the owner of justice.

The facilitator is not a judge.

The facilitator is not a therapist.

The facilitator is not responsible for making participants reconcile.

The facilitator's responsibility is to make the process:

- safe
- bounded
- voluntary where the process requires voluntariness
- procedurally fair
- accessible
- transparent
- interruptible
- reviewable

---

# 1. BOUNDED PROCEDURAL POWER

Justice facilitation necessarily involves procedural power.

The facilitator may:

- establish speaking order
- pause a process
- stop a process
- prevent intimidation
- prevent domination
- separate participants
- require procedural boundaries
- escalate when the case exceeds the role
- document consequential procedural decisions

The facilitator may not:

- determine a person's moral worth
- impose reconciliation
- compel forgiveness
- manufacture consent
- decide factual disputes merely by personal impression
- diagnose participants
- turn bodily observations into findings of fact
- use procedural authority for personal retaliation
- create permanent authority for themselves

> **The facilitator holds power over the process, not sovereignty over the people in it.**

---

# 2. SAFETY BEFORE PROCESS

A restorative process is never more important than immediate safety.

If there is:

- immediate violence
- credible threat
- sexual violence
- serious coercion
- serious child-safety concern
- medical emergency
- inability to participate voluntarily
- severe procedural intimidation

the facilitator may stop the restorative process.

Separation may be the appropriate intervention.

Restorative justice is not synonymous with reconciliation.

---

# 3. PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS

Fairness does not necessarily mean identical treatment.

Relevant questions include:

- Does each participant have meaningful opportunity to speak?
- Can each participant understand the process?
- Can each participant request a pause?
- Are accessibility needs being met?
- Are power differences being addressed?
- Are procedural rules being applied consistently?
- Are relevant differences being recognized rather than erased?

The facilitator should track process rather than attempt to manufacture a predetermined outcome.

---

# 4. OBSERVATION, INTERPRETATION AND EVIDENCE

Facilitators must distinguish:

### Observation
"What I directly observed."

### Participant account
"What a participant says happened."

### Interpretation
"What I think the observation may mean."

### Evidence
"Information relevant to establishing what happened."

### Conclusion
"What the process can responsibly conclude."

These categories must not be collapsed.

For example:

> "The participant stopped speaking and looked down"

is an observation.

> "The participant was afraid"

is an interpretation unless the participant says so.

> "The participant was lying"

is not established merely by the bodily observation.

---

# 5. NERVOUS SYSTEM LITERACY

Facilitators may learn to recognize patterns associated with activation, withdrawal or overwhelm.

Possible signs include:

- raised voice
- rapid speech
- silence
- withdrawal
- agitation
- muscular tension
- changes in breathing
- difficulty following the conversation
- repetitive responses
- visible distress

These signs may be useful information.

They are not a truth detector.

Dysregulation can affect:

- attention
- memory
- communication
- reflection
- capacity to participate safely

Regulation may support:

- attention
- communication
- reflection
- participation

But:

> **A regulated person can lie. A dysregulated person can tell the truth.**

The facilitator must never treat nervous-system state as proof of credibility.

---

# 6. PAUSING

A facilitator may say:

> "I'm pausing the process."

A pause may be appropriate when:

- someone requests it
- communication becomes unsafe
- domination develops
- participants cannot participate voluntarily
- accessibility needs are unmet
- the facilitator is losing procedural clarity
- additional expertise is required

The facilitator should not use "regulation" as a pretext for forcing a participant into a preferred emotional state.

---

# 7. MANIPULATION AND PROCEDURAL DISTORTION

Facilitators should recognize patterns that can distort process.

Examples include:

### The Reasonable One

A participant remains calm and articulate while repeatedly speaking for another person or reframing their account.

Response:

> "Please describe your own experience. Let them describe theirs."

Calmness is not evidence of correctness.

---

### The Complexity Generator

A participant introduces increasing amounts of information to avoid answering a specific question.

Response:

> "I'm going to pause the wider context. Can you answer this specific question first?"

Complexity itself is not manipulation.

The facilitator should focus on observable procedural effects.

---

### The Rule Follower

A participant uses procedural language to dominate the process while appearing compliant.

Response:

> "The structure is being followed formally, but I need to make sure the other participant has meaningful space as well."

---

### The Victim Claimant

A participant repeatedly redirects every question toward their own suffering.

Response:

> "Your experience matters. We also need to stay with the specific issue currently being addressed."

A history of trauma does not remove accountability.

Nor does vulnerability establish wrongdoing.

---

# 8. PREPARATION

Before a session, the facilitator should establish:

- whether participation is voluntary
- whether participants can safely be in the same space
- accessibility requirements
- support persons
- interpretation needs
- relevant procedural boundaries
- escalation pathways
- documentation requirements

Where appropriate, participants should be met separately before a joint process.

---

# 9. OPENING

The facilitator should explain:

- the purpose
- the limits
- the process
- the right to pause
- the right to leave where applicable
- what will be documented
- who can access documentation
- what happens if safety concerns emerge

A useful opening formulation is:

> "I hold the structure of this process. I am not here to decide who you are or to force an outcome. I will intervene when the process becomes unsafe, coercive or procedurally unfair."

---

# 10. PARTICIPANT ACCOUNTS

Each participant should have meaningful space to describe their experience.

Facilitator questions should generally seek clarification rather than cross-examination.

Useful questions include:

- "What happened from your perspective?"
- "What impact did this have on you?"
- "What do you need now?"
- "Is there something you want the other person to understand?"
- "What would repair look like for you?"

The facilitator should not demand a particular emotional presentation.

---

# 11. HARM

The facilitator may help participants distinguish:

- event
- impact
- unmet need
- responsibility
- repair
- future boundary

The process should not reduce harm to moral condemnation.

At the same time, "harm as signal" does not mean harm is unreal or morally irrelevant.

A person can be responsible for harm without being reduced to that harm.

---

# 12. RESTORATION

Possible forms of repair may include:

- acknowledgment
- apology
- changed behaviour
- restitution
- replacement
- practical assistance
- changed boundaries
- separation
- future check-ins

Any contribution described as voluntary must remain genuinely voluntary.

A person should not be socially coerced into providing unpaid emotional, material or relational labour.

Community systems must not depend on voluntary repair contributions for someone's access to the Baseline.

---

# 13. FORGIVENESS

Forgiveness is never a requirement.

A participant may:

- forgive
- not forgive
- remain uncertain
- want distance
- want continued relationship
- end the relationship

Repair does not require reconciliation.

---

# 14. WHEN LEVEL 1 RESTORATIVE PROCESS IS INAPPROPRIATE

Do not proceed with ordinary restorative dialogue when:

- meaningful consent is absent
- there is serious coercion
- safety cannot be maintained
- the harmed person cannot participate freely
- there is serious ongoing violence
- the matter requires specialist intervention
- legal or safeguarding obligations take precedence

The correct response may be:

- protective separation
- specialist referral
- safeguarding intervention
- medical intervention
- legal process
- LOTUS procedure
- other established Flow process

---

# 15. DOCUMENTATION

Documentation is governance infrastructure.

It must be:

- minimal
- purpose-bound
- transparent in scope
- access-limited
- contestable
- subject to expiry where appropriate

A facilitator should record consequential procedural facts without turning personal interpretation into permanent truth.

Where possible, distinguish:

- direct observation
- participant statement
- procedural action
- reason for action
- uncertainty
- follow-up required

---

# 16. REVIEW

A participant should be able to raise concerns about:

- procedural unfairness
- inappropriate intervention
- inadequate accessibility
- misuse of facilitator power
- documentation
- conflicts of interest

Review should examine the process, not merely whether the facilitator intended well.

Good intentions do not eliminate procedural power.

---

# 17. FACILITATOR LIMITS

A Justice Facilitator is not:

- a therapist
- a physician
- a diagnostician
- a police officer
- a lawyer
- a child-protection authority
- an investigator beyond their defined role

Professional or legal procedures must be validated against the relevant jurisdiction and current professional standards.

---

# 18. ROTATION AND SUPERVISION

Justice facilitation should be rotated.

Facilitators should have:

- co-facilitation where appropriate
- peer supervision
- opportunities for debriefing
- clear maximum service periods
- a right to step down

A facilitator who is personally involved in a case should normally recuse themselves.

---

# 19. BURNOUT

Burnout is a system signal, not an individual moral failure.

A facilitator should step back when:

- they cannot maintain procedural clarity
- they are personally activated
- they develop strong attachment to an outcome
- they are exhausted
- they begin treating participants as problems rather than people
- they feel indispensable

The correct response is not to push through.

---

# 20. CORE RULE

> **Justice facilitation protects the process without becoming sovereign over the people inside it.**

The facilitator's task is not to make everyone agree.

It is to maintain a process in which safety, dignity, agency, accountability and separation remain possible.
