
# CLEAN_WATER_HOW_TO.md

# 🌊 Clean Water: A Practical, Locally Replicable Guide

**Purpose:** To help households and Flow Node Circles clarify water, reduce microbial risks, protect treated water from recontamination, and develop reliable local water systems without profit or licensing barriers.

**Principle:** Safe water is a human necessity. Access to safe water must not depend on purchasing power, employment, productivity, or participation in Flow.

**Scope:** This guide covers basic water clarification, emergency household treatment, solar disinfection, safe storage, and the responsible use of filtration materials. It is an introductory guide, not a substitute for water-quality testing or professionally designed community water infrastructure.

---

## 1. Core Principles

Water safety has several distinct dimensions:

1. **Physical quality:** Sand, silt, suspended particles, and other visible material.
2. **Microbiological safety:** Bacteria, viruses, and parasites that can cause disease.
3. **Chemical safety:** Potential contaminants such as arsenic, lead, pesticides, industrial chemicals, and excessive concentrations of naturally occurring substances.
4. **Acceptability:** Taste, odor, color, and other characteristics that affect whether people can comfortably use the water.
5. **Reliable access:** Sufficient quantity, availability when needed, convenient access, and continuity of supply.

These dimensions require different solutions.

Water that looks clear is not necessarily safe. Water that tastes normal is not necessarily safe. A filter that removes particles may leave pathogens and dissolved chemicals behind.

**The objective is verified safe water, not merely clear water.**

### A critical distinction

Household clarification and disinfection can make some microbiologically contaminated water safe to drink when the source and treatment method are appropriate.

They cannot make every possible source safe.

Water contaminated with fuel, toxic chemicals, radioactive substances, or unknown industrial pollutants must not be assumed safe after boiling, solar disinfection, or ordinary household filtration.

When such contamination is suspected, use a verified alternative source and obtain advice from the relevant water-quality authority.

## 2. Choosing the Appropriate Method

| Water condition | Appropriate response | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Water contains visible sand or silt | Allow settling, then decant and prefilter | Does not establish microbiological safety |
| Water may contain disease-causing microorganisms | Use a validated disinfection method, such as boiling | Does not remove chemical contamination |
| Clear water is available and sunlight conditions are suitable | Solar disinfection (SODIS) may be used according to established instructions | Requires suitable bottles, sufficiently clear water, and appropriate sunlight |
| Chemical contamination is possible | Test the water and select treatment for the identified contaminant | Generic sand filters, boiling, and SODIS are insufficient |
| Water has an unknown source or unknown contamination | Use a verified safe source until the risk is assessed | Appearance and smell cannot establish safety |
| A community needs a continuous, large-volume supply | Assess the source, treatment train, storage, distribution, and monitoring requirements | A small improvised bottle filter is not a community water plant |

**Preferred approach:** Protect a safe source first. Treatment is a second line of protection, not a reason to ignore source contamination.

## 3. Materials for Basic Clarification

A simple gravity filter can reduce suspended particles before disinfection.

Possible materials include:

- A clean, food-safe container or purpose-built filter housing
- Washed gravel or small stones
- Washed coarse sand
- Washed fine sand
- Clean cloth or a suitable prefilter
- A separate clean container for collecting clarified water
- A lid for protecting collected water
- A suitable method of disinfection, if required

Use materials that have not been exposed to pesticides, fuel, industrial chemicals, paint, or other hazardous substances.

Do not use containers that previously held toxic chemicals.

Avoid unknown plastics, treated wood, painted materials, and other materials that could release contaminants into water.

### Preparing the filter media

1. Wash the gravel and sand thoroughly with water known to be safe.
2. Remove leaves, organic debris, and visible foreign material.
3. Use clean containers and equipment.
4. Assemble the filter so that the media cannot escape into the collection container.
5. Discard the first filtrate if necessary to remove loose dust, following the filter design's instructions.

The filter should be stable, easy to clean, and protected from contamination during use.

## 4. Building a Simple Clarification Filter

This design is intended to reduce visible particles. **It is not, by itself, a validated drinking-water treatment system.**

### Construction

1. Use a clean, food-safe container designed to hold the filter media.
2. Install a clean outlet or collection arrangement that prevents gravel and sand from escaping.
3. Place washed gravel in the supporting layer.
4. Place washed coarse sand above the gravel.
5. Place washed fine sand above the coarse sand.
6. If useful, place a clean, removable prefilter at the inlet to catch larger debris.
7. Direct the outlet into a separate, clean collection container.
8. Test the system for leaks, clogging, and loss of filter material.

The exact arrangement and depth of each layer depend on the filter design. A household bottle assembled from improvised materials should not be treated as equivalent to an engineered slow-sand or biosand filter.

### What this filter can and cannot do

It can help remove suspended particles and improve clarity.

Its effectiveness against microorganisms depends on the actual filter design, construction, operation, and maintenance. An improvised sand-and-gravel filter cannot be assumed to remove bacteria, viruses, or parasites reliably.

It does not reliably remove dissolved salts, heavy metals, pesticides, or other dissolved chemicals.

**Never use visual clarity as proof that the water is safe to drink.**

## 5. Method A: Boiling Water

Boiling is an established way to inactivate disease-causing bacteria, viruses, and parasites.

It is particularly useful for household quantities when the water is not suspected of chemical contamination.

### Step-by-step procedure

1. If the water is cloudy, allow suspended material to settle.
2. Carefully pour off the clearer water without disturbing the sediment.
3. If needed, pass it through a clean cloth or suitable prefilter to remove remaining particles.
4. Heat the water until it reaches a vigorous rolling boil.
5. Maintain the rolling boil for **at least one minute**.
6. At elevations above approximately 2,000 metres, maintain the rolling boil for **three minutes**.
7. Allow the water to cool naturally in a clean, covered container.
8. Protect the water from hands, dirty utensils, dust, animals, and other sources of contamination.

Do not add untreated water to water that has already been boiled.

### Limitations

Boiling:

- Inactivates disease-causing microorganisms when carried out correctly.
- Does not remove dissolved heavy metals, pesticides, salts, fuel, or most other chemical contaminants.
- Does not reliably remove all substances that cause unpleasant taste or odor.
- Requires an energy source and a safe way to heat and cool the water.

Boiling can concentrate some non-volatile dissolved contaminants as water evaporates. Do not boil water of uncertain chemical quality in an attempt to make it safe.

### Fire and burn safety

Use a stable heat source in a well-ventilated area. Keep children away from hot vessels and boiling water. Allow water to cool before transferring or drinking it.

## 6. Method B: Solar Water Disinfection (SODIS)

Solar water disinfection uses sunlight to reduce microbial contamination in suitable water.

It is a low-energy option for small quantities when the water source and local conditions are appropriate.

### Materials

- Clean, transparent PET drinking-water bottles
- Water that has been clarified sufficiently for sunlight to penetrate
- Direct sunlight
- A location where the bottles can remain undisturbed

Do not use opaque containers, colored bottles, or bottles that block the required sunlight. Do not use damaged, heavily scratched, or dirty bottles.

### Step-by-step procedure

1. Inspect the source. Do not use water suspected of chemical contamination.
2. If the water is cloudy, allow it to settle and prefilter it until it is sufficiently clear.
3. Fill clean, transparent PET bottles with the clarified water.
4. Close the bottles securely.
5. Lay the bottles horizontally in direct sunlight.
6. Expose them for **at least six hours in sunny conditions**.
7. Under heavily cloudy conditions, the established SODIS guidance recommends **two consecutive days of exposure**.
8. If conditions are outside the method's operating limits, use another validated treatment method.
9. Protect treated bottles from recontamination and consume or store the water appropriately.

Follow the established SODIS guidance for bottle suitability, water clarity, exposure conditions, and local climate.

### Important limitations

SODIS:

- Can reduce microbial risks when its operating conditions are met.
- Does not remove dissolved chemical contaminants.
- Is less effective when suspended particles prevent sunlight from reaching microorganisms.
- Depends on sunlight, bottle condition, exposure time, water clarity, and the organisms present.
- Is not a universal substitute for testing or a reliable piped water supply.

Do not assume that combining SODIS with boiling automatically provides a meaningful additional safety benefit. Select a suitable treatment process and follow its instructions correctly.

## 7. Method C: Activated Carbon

Activated carbon is a specialized filter medium with a large internal surface area.

It can adsorb certain organic compounds and may improve taste and odor. Depending on the carbon and filter design, it can also reduce some other contaminants.

However, its performance is specific to the contaminant, carbon type, contact time, water chemistry, and filter design.

### What activated carbon may do

A suitable activated-carbon filter may reduce:

- Some organic chemicals
- Some compounds responsible for taste and odor
- Chlorine in water where chlorine removal is appropriate

### What it cannot be assumed to do

Activated carbon is not a universal solution for:

- Bacteria, viruses, or parasites
- All heavy metals
- All pesticides or industrial chemicals
- Dissolved salts
- Radioactive contamination

A reduction in one contaminant does not prove that other contaminants have been removed.

### Recommended use

1. Prefer activated carbon intended and tested for drinking-water treatment.
2. Use a filter whose manufacturer identifies the contaminants it is designed to reduce.
3. Follow the specified flow rate, service life, and replacement schedule.
4. Replace the medium when required, rather than relying on appearance, smell, or taste.
5. If the filter is used for a known chemical hazard, verify its performance for that specific contaminant.
6. Treat microbiological risks separately when necessary.

Activated carbon has a finite adsorption capacity. Once its effective capacity is exhausted, contaminant removal can decline even when water still flows normally.

### About making activated carbon locally

Ordinary charcoal and activated carbon are not interchangeable.

Producing charcoal by heating wood with limited oxygen does not automatically produce activated carbon. Activation normally requires controlled processing, and the finished material must be suitable for its intended application.

Soaking homemade charcoal in calcium chloride, lemon juice, or vinegar does not establish that it has been properly activated or made safe for drinking-water treatment.

A simple food-coloring test cannot establish drinking-water safety or demonstrate removal of hazardous chemicals.

For these reasons, this guide does **not** recommend homemade chemical activation as a validated drinking-water treatment method.

Local production research may be worthwhile, but the material must be characterized and tested before it is used to supply drinking water.

### Other uses

Carbonaceous materials may have other applications, including soil improvement and odor control. Material intended for those uses must not automatically be reused in drinking-water systems.

Do not use homemade charcoal as a medical treatment for poisoning. Suspected poisoning requires immediate professional advice.

## 8. Chemical Contamination: A Separate Problem

Chemical safety cannot be established through a generic household filter.

Potential hazards include:

- Arsenic and other toxic metals
- Lead from pipes or fittings
- Pesticides and industrial chemicals
- Fuel and petroleum products
- Excessive fluoride or other naturally occurring substances
- Salinity and dissolved salts
- Radioactive substances

Different contaminants require different treatment technologies. Depending on the hazard, these may include specialized adsorption media, ion exchange, membrane treatment, precipitation, or other engineered processes.

No single technology removes every contaminant.

### Safe procedure when chemical contamination is suspected

1. Stop using the source for drinking and food preparation.
2. Identify the likely contamination source.
3. Obtain water from a verified safe alternative.
4. Arrange appropriate water-quality testing.
5. Select treatment based on the actual contaminants and measured concentrations.
6. Verify the treated water before declaring it safe.
7. Monitor the source and treatment system over time.

Do not attempt to solve an unknown chemical problem by combining several improvised filters.

**A safe water system begins with understanding the source.**

## 9. Safe Water Storage

Treated water can become contaminated again during cooling, transport, pouring, or storage.

Safe storage is therefore part of the treatment process.

### Choosing a container

Use a clean, durable container made from material suitable for drinking water.

Prefer a container with:

- A tight-fitting lid
- A narrow opening or a protected dispensing tap
- A design that prevents hands and utensils from entering the water
- Sufficient strength for regular use and cleaning

Never use a container that previously held fuel, pesticides, industrial chemicals, or other toxic substances.

### Cleaning and handling

1. Wash hands before handling treated water or clean containers.
2. Clean containers regularly.
3. Sanitize containers when appropriate using an established procedure and a suitable product.
4. Follow the product's instructions and local public-health guidance.
5. Keep containers covered.
6. Pour water without touching the inside of the container or lid.
7. Use a clean dispensing tap or dedicated clean utensil where appropriate.
8. Keep stored water away from fuel, chemicals, waste, animals, and other contamination sources.

Do not rinse a sanitized container with unsafe water.

### Storage and labeling

- Mark containers intended for drinking water.
- Record the treatment date where useful.
- Protect stored water from contamination and excessive heat.
- Clean and inspect containers before refilling.
- Do not mix untreated water with treated water.

Safe storage procedures should be adapted to the treatment method and local conditions.

## 10. Maintenance and Quality Control

A water system is not safe simply because it worked once.

Every system requires maintenance, monitoring, and a clear response when something goes wrong.

### Routine checks

Record:

- The water source
- The date and time of collection
- The treatment method used
- Relevant operating conditions
- Filter cleaning and replacement
- Disinfection procedures
- Any unusual color, smell, or taste
- Known incidents or suspected contamination
- Test results, where available

These records help a community identify changes and maintain reliable operation.

### When to stop using a system

Stop using the system for drinking water if:

- Chemical contamination is suspected.
- The source has changed unexpectedly.
- The treatment process has failed.
- A filter is damaged or used outside its specifications.
- The water has been exposed to a known contamination event.
- Testing indicates that the water does not meet the applicable safety requirements.

Use a verified alternative until the problem is resolved.

Clear appearance, normal taste, and the absence of illness are not substitutes for water-quality testing.

### Community testing

A community water system should have a plan for appropriate testing, including microbiological indicators and chemical parameters relevant to the source.

Testing frequency depends on source type, contamination risks, treatment technology, system size, and local requirements.

Where possible, establish a relationship with a competent water laboratory or public-health authority.

## 11. From Household Filters to Community Water Systems

A household filter and a community water supply solve different problems.

For a community, the objective is not merely to produce one container of clean water. It is to provide enough safe water reliably, over time, to everyone who needs it.

A community system may require:

1. Source assessment and protection.
2. Sustainable abstraction or collection.
3. Pretreatment and removal of suspended solids.
4. Treatment for relevant microbiological and chemical hazards.
5. Appropriate storage.
6. Distribution that protects water quality.
7. Accessible collection points or household connections.
8. Reliable energy where required.
9. Spare parts, tools, and maintenance capacity.
10. Water-quality monitoring and incident response.
11. Training and shared documentation.
12. A plan for droughts, floods, equipment failure, and other disruptions.

The appropriate design depends on local geology, climate, population, settlement patterns, source quality, available skills, and the required volume of water.

Small decentralized systems may be suitable in some settings. Larger systems or connected networks may be more appropriate in others. A combination may be best.

**Local autonomy does not require every community to use the same technology. It requires the capacity to choose, operate, maintain, and verify a suitable system.**

## 12. Flow Node Implementation

Flow Node Circles can support safe water access through shared knowledge, local maintenance, transparent records, and cooperation.

### Suggested implementation sequence

#### Stage 1: Understand the source

- Identify possible water sources.
- Document seasonal changes and known contamination risks.
- Determine whether the source has existing water-quality data.
- Identify the competent local authority or laboratory.

#### Stage 2: Establish immediate safe access

- Identify a verified safe source or an appropriate validated treatment method.
- Make clear instructions available in accessible formats.
- Ensure that water is available to people who cannot transport or treat it themselves.
- Do not require people to prove productivity or participation to receive safe water.

#### Stage 3: Select the technology

- Match the treatment method to the water-quality assessment.
- Consider energy, water volume, accessibility, maintenance, and replacement materials.
- Compare the total lifecycle cost rather than only the initial construction cost.
- Avoid declaring a design safe before its performance has been established.

#### Stage 4: Train and document

- Teach operators how to use the system correctly.
- Document the design, operating limits, and maintenance procedures.
- Keep replacement materials and tools available.
- Record faults and corrective actions.
- Share lessons with other communities without presenting untested designs as proven solutions.

#### Stage 5: Verify and improve

- Test water at appropriate intervals.
- Investigate failures rather than concealing them.
- Update procedures when better evidence becomes available.
- Maintain alternative supplies for interruptions and emergencies.

### Guiding principles

- Knowledge should be freely shareable.
- Designs should be reproducible where technically appropriate.
- No single supplier should have unnecessary control over essential water access.
- Local communities should have meaningful control over their systems.
- Accessibility is part of system design, not an optional addition.
- Technical claims must be supported by evidence.
- Water-quality failures must trigger protection and repair, not blame.

## 13. Water Quantity and the Baseline

Water quality and water quantity are related but distinct requirements.

A supply may be microbiologically safe and still be insufficient for human needs. Conversely, a large volume of water is not beneficial if it is unsafe.

Flow can use **100 litres per person per day as a planning reference for substantial household water access**, provided the document makes clear what the figure includes and how it will be supplied.

It should not be interpreted as a universal minimum established by this guide, a compulsory personal consumption target, or a guarantee that every local source can sustain that withdrawal.

Planning must account for:

- Drinking and food preparation
- Personal hygiene
- Laundry and cleaning
- Sanitation
- Household size and specific needs
- Health, disability, and accessibility
- Climate and seasonal conditions
- Water availability and ecosystem requirements
- Treatment losses and distribution losses
- The needs of future users

Water systems must be designed around sustainable source capacity and actual human needs. A target volume must not justify depletion of aquifers, rivers, wetlands, or other ecosystems.

The objective is sufficient, safe, accessible, and dependable water for everyone.

## 14. Safety Rules at a Glance

1. **Clear water is not necessarily safe water.**
2. **Sand, gravel, and cloth are clarification materials, not a complete guarantee of drinking-water safety.**
3. **Boiling addresses microorganisms, not chemical contamination.**
4. **SODIS is conditional on water clarity, bottle suitability, sunlight, and exposure time.**
5. **Activated carbon is contaminant-specific and has a finite service life.**
6. **Homemade charcoal is not automatically activated carbon.**
7. **Unknown chemical contamination requires a different source or appropriate testing and treatment.**
8. **Treated water must be protected from recontamination.**
9. **A community supply needs monitoring, maintenance, and contingency planning.**
10. **Never claim that water is safe without an adequate basis for that claim.**

## Final Principle

Safe water should not be a privilege reserved for people who can pay.

It is possible to share knowledge, use locally appropriate materials, build community capacity, and reduce dependence on exclusive suppliers. Those goals become stronger when technical limits are stated honestly and water quality is verified.

**The best water system is not simply the one that is easiest to build. It is the one that reliably provides safe, sufficient, accessible water and can be maintained by the people who depend on it.**

**LIFE > BASELINE > FLOW**
