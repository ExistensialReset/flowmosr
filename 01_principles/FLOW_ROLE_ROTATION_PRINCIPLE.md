# FLOW ROLE ROTATION PRINCIPLE

**Status:** CORE PRINCIPLE  
**Layer:** `01_principles`  
**Scope:** All Flow roles, functions, panels, mandates, representations and delegated responsibilities  
**Principle:** No Flow role is permanent.

---

## 1. PURPOSE

Flow distributes responsibility across people without allowing responsibility to become permanent personal power.

A role may need continuity.

A function may need stability.

Knowledge may need to accumulate.

Experience may need to deepen.

But no person should become the permanent holder of a Flow function.

The purpose of rotation is therefore not constant disruption.

It is to prevent a temporary function from becoming a personal position.

> **The function may be stable. The person is not the function.**

Rotation is a structural safeguard against:

- concentration of power
- dependency on individuals
- informal hierarchy
- expertise becoming authority
- institutional capture
- founder dependence
- gatekeeping
- permanent interpretation rights
- personal ownership of collective functions

---

# 2. SCOPE

This principle applies to **all Flow roles**.

It applies regardless of:

- the name of the role
- the level of Flow
- the geographical location
- the technical difficulty
- the amount of responsibility
- whether the role is selected by LOTUS
- whether the role is delegated by a Circle
- whether the role is elected, selected, invited or randomly assigned
- whether the role is visible or mostly administrative
- whether the role exists in Governance, Justice, Facilitation, Human Mirrors, technical infrastructure or external interfaces

Examples include, but are not limited to:

- Facilitators
- Human Mirrors
- LOTUS panelists
- Justice roles
- Governance roles
- Node roles
- Technical Guardians
- Information Stewards
- External Interface roles
- Review and audit roles
- Child Witness specialists
- temporary coordinators
- delegated representatives
- emergency functions

No category of role becomes permanent merely because it is important.

No category of role becomes exempt from rotation merely because it is difficult.

---

# 3. THE NINE-MONTH PRINCIPLE

A normal Flow mandate may last **up to nine months**.

Nine months is a maximum, not a requirement.

A role may have a shorter mandate when that better serves:

- safety
- continuity
- accessibility
- workload
- learning
- local circumstances
- the nature of the function

No role should automatically be extended simply because nine months have not yet passed.

Likewise, reaching nine months does not mean that an active process must be abandoned without a safe transition.

> **Nine months is a ceiling for ordinary mandates, not a promise of uninterrupted service.**

---

# 4. COOLDOWN

After completing a mandate, a person should normally have a **minimum cooldown period of two months before taking the same role again**.

Cooldown exists to create actual distance between periods of authority.

It is not punishment.

It is not a judgment about the person.

It is a structural interval.

The person remains a full participant in Flow during cooldown.

They may:

- participate in Circles
- contribute knowledge
- help others learn
- offer feedback
- take other roles when permitted
- question existing processes
- participate in ordinary collective life

They simply do not immediately return to the same function.

> **Cooldown separates participation from repeated authority.**

---

# 5. ROTATION IS NOT ARBITRARY REPLACEMENT

Rotation does not mean that a person must be removed from an active responsibility at exactly the moment a calendar period ends.

A role may sometimes require continuity.

For example:

- an active Justice process may still be underway
- a safeguarding process may require continuity
- a technical transition may not yet be complete
- an external obligation may have a fixed endpoint
- an emergency may make immediate replacement unsafe

In such cases, a temporary continuation or structured handoff may be necessary.

But continuity must not become a hidden route around rotation.

Any continuation beyond the ordinary mandate should therefore be:

- specific
- justified
- limited
- documented
- reviewable
- connected to an existing process or concrete transition

It should end when the continuity need ends.

> **Continuity protects people from the system. Rotation protects people from accumulated power.**

Both are necessary.

---

# 6. NO PERMANENT EXCEPTIONS

A role must not become permanently exempt from rotation because:

- only one person currently knows how to perform it
- that person is exceptionally competent
- replacing them feels inconvenient
- training others takes time
- the role is considered too important
- the person has become trusted
- the community has become dependent on them
- nobody else currently volunteers
- the function has gradually become associated with one individual

If a role repeatedly cannot rotate, the problem is not automatically the people.

The problem may be the design of the function.

> **A function that cannot rotate is a function whose design must be questioned.**

---

# 7. REDESIGN BEFORE EXEMPTION

When there are too few people able or willing to rotate through a role, Flow should first examine whether the role itself can be redesigned.

Possible responses include:

- reducing the scope of the function
- dividing the function into several roles
- creating paired responsibility
- creating apprenticeship or shadowing
- simplifying procedures
- improving documentation
- making training accessible
- separating specialist knowledge from decision authority
- automating routine tasks where appropriate
- reducing unnecessary requirements
- creating a larger eligible pool
- changing the frequency of rotation
- changing the function itself

The default response to a small qualified pool is therefore **structural adaptation**, not permanent exemption.

> **When the structure cannot rotate the people, change the structure.**

---

# 8. KNOWLEDGE MAY CONTINUE

Rotation does not require collective amnesia.

A person leaving a role may continue to contribute knowledge in appropriate ways.

Documentation should make it possible for the next person to understand:

- what has happened
- what remains unfinished
- what decisions have been made
- what uncertainties remain
- what risks are known
- what procedures are relevant
- what lessons were learned

Knowledge should therefore be transferable.

Experience should be shareable.

Training should be encouraged.

But authority must not be inherited automatically from the previous role-holder.

> **Knowledge may persist. Authority does not.**

A person who previously held a role does not automatically retain decision-making power over that function.

---

# 9. NO PERSONAL INTERPRETIVE MONOPOLY

Rotation is also necessary where the main power is not formal decision-making but interpretation.

A person may become influential by becoming:

- the person everyone asks
- the person who knows all the precedents
- the person who explains what Flow "really means"
- the person who knows how a protocol is "supposed" to work
- the person who interprets difficult cases
- the person who trains every successor

This can create informal permanence even when formal roles rotate.

Therefore:

> **No person should become the permanent interpreter of a Flow function, protocol or principle.**

Important knowledge should be documented and teachable.

Interpretations should remain open to examination.

Expertise should inform decisions without becoming private authority.

---

# 10. EXPERTISE IS NOT AUTHORITY

Some Flow functions will require significant expertise.

Rotation does not mean pretending that expertise is unnecessary.

Instead, expertise should be separated from permanent authority.

An expert may:

- explain
- advise
- train
- identify risks
- provide technical information
- challenge an assumption
- help another person understand a complex issue

But expertise alone does not create sovereignty over the people affected by the function.

> **Expertise ≠ Authority.**

Where specialist knowledge is genuinely necessary, Flow should seek ways to make that knowledge:

- distributed
- documented
- teachable
- reviewable
- challengeable
- accessible to more than one person

---

# 11. REPEATED SERVICE

Rotation must also address repeated selection.

A person may be selected repeatedly because they are:

- trusted
- experienced
- knowledgeable
- available
- unusually suited to a function

Repeated selection is not automatically evidence of wrongdoing.

But repeated selection can create structural dependency.

Therefore Flow should distinguish between:

**being repeatedly trusted**

and

**becoming structurally indispensable**.

If the same people repeatedly return to the same function, the system should examine:

- whether the eligible pool is too small
- whether training is insufficient
- whether selection criteria are unintentionally narrowing
- whether social reputation is becoming a hidden selection mechanism
- whether the function has become unnecessarily specialized
- whether the role itself should be redesigned

The goal is not to punish competence.

The goal is to prevent competence from becoming ownership.

---

# 12. MULTIPLE ROLES AND COMBINED POWER

Rotation must not be considered only one role at a time.

A person may hold several different temporary functions whose combination creates substantial influence.

For example, one person should not become effectively permanent by rotating between closely connected functions without meaningful separation.

Flow should therefore examine:

- concurrent mandates
- sequential related roles
- Justice and Governance overlap
- technical and decision-making overlap
- review and implementation overlap
- external representation combined with internal authority
- repeated membership in the same decision ecosystem

The question is not only:

> "Has this person rotated?"

The question is also:

> **"Has power actually rotated?"**

---

# 13. ROTATION AND JUSTICE

Justice functions are subject to the same principle.

Justice may require:

- continuity
- specialist knowledge
- safeguarding competence
- procedural experience
- careful case handling

These needs do not make Justice roles permanent.

A Justice role may continue temporarily when necessary for continuity of an active process, but such continuation must not become an indefinite personal mandate.

Justice must also guard against a subtler form of accumulation:

> becoming the person who is always trusted to interpret harm, credibility, evidence or process.

Rotation therefore applies not only to formal Justice decision-makers but, where appropriate, to supporting and interpretive functions as well.

No person becomes the permanent source of truth about another person merely by repeatedly serving in Justice.

---

# 14. ROTATION AND GOVERNANCE

Governance functions are subject to the same principle.

LOTUS, Nodes, Circles, coordinators, representatives, auditors and other governance functions must not develop permanent office-holders.

Rotation should be structural rather than dependent on the goodwill of current leaders.

No person should have to "approve" the rotation of another person in order for rotation to occur.

The system should make rotation ordinary.

> **Rotation should be a property of the structure, not a favor granted by a person.**

---

# 15. ROTATION AND EMERGENCY

Emergency situations may require temporary continuation or immediate reassignment.

Safety takes precedence over calendar neatness.

However, emergency powers must not silently create permanent authority.

Emergency continuation should therefore have:

- a defined reason
- a defined scope
- a defined review point
- a defined end condition
- a handoff plan where possible

Emergency conditions should not become a permanent justification for abandoning rotation.

> **An emergency may interrupt rotation. It may not abolish rotation.**

---

# 16. HANDOFF

When a person leaves a role, the transition should preserve necessary continuity without transferring personal authority.

A handoff may include:

- current status
- unresolved questions
- relevant documentation
- known risks
- pending deadlines
- active safeguards
- technical information
- lessons learned

The departing person may explain the situation.

The incoming person must remain free to examine it independently.

A handoff is therefore a transfer of **context**, not a transfer of **ownership**.

> **The next person inherits the work, not the previous person's authority.**

---

# 17. ACCESSIBILITY

Rotation must not be designed in a way that excludes people because of disability, communication style, energy level, caregiving responsibilities, geography or other access needs.

If a role can only be rotated among a narrow group because the function is inaccessible, Flow should first ask:

> **What can be changed about the function?**

Possible adaptations include:

- assistive technology
- asynchronous participation
- distributed tasks
- shorter sessions
- paired roles
- flexible timing
- remote participation
- accessible documentation
- alternative communication methods
- reduced administrative burden

Accessibility is therefore part of rotation architecture.

A role that is technically "open to everyone" but practically accessible only to a few is not fully rotatable.

---

# 18. ROTATION MUST NOT BECOME BUREAUCRACY

Rotation is a safeguard, not an end in itself.

Flow should not create unnecessary administrative machinery simply to prove that rotation has happened.

The system should avoid:

- excessive tracking
- unnecessary personal records
- constant reporting
- surveillance of participants
- complicated eligibility bureaucracy
- artificial role changes with no meaningful change in power

The test is practical:

> **Does this structure actually distribute power?**

If not, simplify it.

---

# 19. WHAT ROTATION PROTECTS

Rotation protects both the community and the individual.

It protects the community from:

- capture
- dependency
- gatekeeping
- informal hierarchy
- institutional memory becoming personal property
- concentration of interpretation
- closed professional classes

It protects individuals from:

- becoming indispensable
- carrying responsibility indefinitely
- being trapped in a role
- being treated as the permanent expert
- becoming the target of accumulated expectations
- having their identity reduced to a function

A person should be able to leave a role without losing their belonging.

---

# 20. WHAT ROTATION DOES NOT MEAN

Rotation does **not** mean:

- everyone has equal expertise
- every role must have identical mandates
- every person must perform every function
- experienced people must become silent
- knowledge must be discarded
- continuity is unimportant
- competence is suspicious
- people must be replaced during active emergencies
- people cannot help train successors
- people cannot return to a role after cooldown
- all functions must be performed by lottery
- formal rotation automatically eliminates informal power

Rotation is one structural safeguard among several.

It must work together with:

- cognitive ownership
- non-coercion
- Baseline protection
- structural impartiality
- decentralization
- transparency without surveillance
- accessibility
- recusal
- appeal
- documentation
- distributed competence
- adaptation

---

# 21. THE CORE TEST

Whenever a Flow role is designed, ask:

### Can another person perform this function?

If yes:

**How is the knowledge transferred?**

If no:

**Why not?**

If the answer is:

> "Because this person knows how."

then ask:

**Why does only one person know how?**

If the answer is:

> "Because the function is too specialized."

then ask:

**Can the function be redesigned so that the knowledge is distributed?**

If the answer is:

> "Because we trust this person."

then ask:

**Can trust remain without permanent authority?**

If the answer is:

> "Because nobody else wants to do it."

then ask:

**What does the function need to become in order to be shareable?**

---

# 22. META-PRINCIPLE

Rotation should not merely move people through existing structures.

It should continuously test whether those structures are still necessary in their current form.

If a function repeatedly requires the same person, Flow should investigate the dependency.

If a function repeatedly requires exceptional expertise, Flow should investigate the design.

If a function repeatedly accumulates informal authority, Flow should investigate the power structure.

If rotation itself becomes burdensome, Flow should investigate the rotation mechanism.

> **Nothing in Flow should become permanent merely because it has existed for a long time.**

---

# 23. THE PRINCIPLE IN ONE SENTENCE

> **No Flow role is permanent; ordinary mandates may last up to nine months, followed by at least two months of cooldown before returning to the same role, and when a function cannot be meaningfully rotated, the function itself must be redesigned rather than permanently exempted.**

---

# 24. FINAL PRINCIPLES

**The function can be stable. The person is not the function.**

**Nine months is a ceiling, not an entitlement.**

**Cooldown separates participation from repeated authority.**

**Continuity may justify temporary continuation, but never permanent exemption.**

**Knowledge may persist. Authority does not.**

**Expertise is not authority.**

**Trust is not ownership.**

**Rotation must distribute actual power, not merely change titles.**

**A function that cannot rotate is a function whose design must be questioned.**

**When the structure cannot rotate the people, change the structure.**

**No person should become indispensable to Flow.**

**No Flow role is permanent.**
